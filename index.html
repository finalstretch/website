The problem

Governments publish a huge amount of useful data: clinical trials, drug recalls, inspection records, court dockets. Almost all of it is written for researchers, regulators, and professionals, not for the people it’s actually about.

The information is out there. It just never quite reaches the person who needs it. We’re trying to build that last stretch: apps you can use in plain language, at the moment you actually need them.

Non-negotiable

Our commitments

We wrote these down before we wrote any code, and they don’t change.

Free

No price, no in-app purchases, no paid tiers. Everyone gets the whole app.

No advertising

No ads, ever. And no analytics built to sell you to someone else.

No data collection

What you type stays on your phone. Our apps have no accounts, no logins, and no servers of our own. Your searches go straight from your phone to the public data source.

No interested funding

We don’t take money from anyone with a stake in what you see. For health apps, that means no trial sponsors, no recruitment companies, no drug makers.

How we decide what to build

Does this move the data closer to the person, or move the person closer to the data?

A search form that expects you to know the right medical term fails that test. Letting you type what you’d actually say, and translating it, passes. If a feature only makes sense to someone who already knows the dataset, we don’t build it.

What we’re building

The apps

In testing

Trialhead

A clinical trial finder in your words

Searching for a clinical trial shouldn’t require learning how ClinicalTrials.gov works. Trialhead lets you search in plain words, explains the eligibility criteria in plain language, and helps you get ready to talk to your doctor about it.

There are other trial-search services out there, but most are funded by trial sponsors and patient recruitment companies, so they have an interest in what you see. Nobody pays us to show you anything.

Trialhead never tells you whether you qualify for a trial. That decision belongs to you, your doctor, and the study team. The app finds trials, explains them, and points you to the study contact. It is not medical advice. Privacy · Support

In testing

Rxcall

Recall alerts for the medications you actually take

Drug recalls come out every week, but they’re announced to pharmacies and regulators, and most people never hear about the ones that affect them. Rxcall keeps your medication list on your phone and checks it against public FDA recall data, so the one recall that matters to you actually reaches you.

Your medication list never leaves your phone. If something you take is recalled, Rxcall shows you what the recall says and points you to your pharmacist or doctor. It never tells you to stop or change a medication. Privacy · Support

Exploring

What’s next

We keep a list of other places where public data doesn’t reach people: choosing a nursing home in a hurry, dealing with a court case without a lawyer, transit apps that don’t know the station elevator is broken.

Know a dataset that deserves the same treatment? Tell us about it.

Open source

Everything we ship will be open source under the Apache 2.0 license. Once the apps are out, you’ll be able to read every line of code that runs on your phone and check that it does what we say.

We think privacy promises should be checkable, not just something you have to believe.

We’re at github.com/finalstretch. The app repos are private while we test, and we’ll open them up when the apps reach the App Store.

Who builds this

Who we are

We’re Ruhi and Reeha, two siblings building our first apps. Each of us owns one app, and neither of us can ship a change alone: everything gets reviewed and approved before it merges, starting with whichever of us didn’t write it.

Before we started, we signed an agreement with each other locking in the commitments above. There are no investors, no sponsors, and nothing for sale.

When the apps reach the App Store, we’ll publish the code, so you won’t have to take any of this on faith.
